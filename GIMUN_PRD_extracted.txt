2. Executive Summary
The Organizing Committee runs two respected, competitive student events on the same campus, in the same window of days: GIMUN, a Model United Nations conference where students simulate diplomatic negotiation across UN-style committees, and the GIKI Moot Cup, a moot court competition where law and non-law students argue simulated legal cases before a bench of judges. Today, information about both events is scattered across Facebook posts, WhatsApp groups, and PDF attachments — delegates and moot participants have no single, authoritative place to find registration details, committee or case assignments, background guides, the day-by-day schedule, or venue logistics. That gap creates avoidable confusion for participants, extra manual work for organizers answering the same questions repeatedly, and a less credible first impression for sponsors and faculty advisors evaluating whether to support or send delegations to the event.
This PRD specifies a single, custom-built website that solves that problem by becoming the definitive hub for both events. It is designed around four priorities the Organizing Committee identified as most important: a simple, no-payment registration flow that captures the information organizers need and lets the team follow up manually; a content hub that puts every handbook, background guide, and schedule in one browsable, searchable place instead of buried in file attachments; live-day features so announcements, schedule changes, and results reach participants instantly instead of through word-of-mouth; and a sponsor and media showcase that gives partners a professional, credible reason to say yes.
The site is scoped for a medium-sized combined event (roughly 150–400 participants across both tracks), built entirely in custom code, maintained by one person on the Organizing Committee, and delivered in English only. Because the two events share dates and a venue but have very different content (committees and country assignments for GIMUN; problem categories, memorials, and bench panels for the Moot Cup), the site's information architecture treats them as two clearly branded tracks under one shared shell — shared homepage, shared venue/travel/FAQ/sponsor content, but dedicated sections, registration forms, and resource libraries for each event. This avoids the two most common failure modes in joint-event sites: forcing one event to feel like an afterthought bolted onto the other, or duplicating so much shared content that it becomes inconsistent and hard to maintain.
The recommended technical approach favors simplicity over cleverness, in direct response to the fact that one person will maintain this codebase without a team behind them: a lightweight, file-based content system (content lives in structured Markdown/JSON files inside the codebase, edited directly and redeployed) rather than a full database-backed admin panel, unless the Organizing Committee's technical comfort and available time say otherwise (see Section 13 for both paths). This keeps ongoing maintenance realistic for a single, likely time-constrained, student maintainer while still producing a professional, fast, modern website.
This document is organized to be read start-to-finish by the person building the site, or dipped into by section during development. It totals the full feature set, content architecture, design direction, non-functional requirements, delivery timeline, risks, and a launch checklist, so that by the end there is nothing about the website left ambiguous except the three explicitly flagged open items (final event names/edition, exact dates, and budget), which are simple placeholders to swap once confirmed.
3. Introduction & Background
3.1 What GIMUN Is
A Model United Nations (MUN) conference is an educational simulation of the real United Nations and its bodies. Students, called delegates, are each assigned a country, organization, or historical figure to represent inside a committee — a simulated version of a real UN body such as the General Assembly, the Security Council, or a specialized agency like UNHCR or WHO. Within committee sessions, delegates research their assigned country's actual foreign policy positions, then debate, negotiate, form blocs, draft working papers, and ultimately vote on resolutions addressing real global issues, all while following a structured set of parliamentary procedure rules (motions, points of order, formal and informal caucusing) that keep debate organized. Delegates are evaluated by committee chairs on diplomacy, research quality, public speaking, negotiation skill, and adherence to their assigned country's real position — awards typically include Best Delegate, Outstanding Delegate, and Honorable Mention per committee.
GIMUN follows this same format: delegates apply or register (individually or as part of a school/university delegation), are allocated to a committee and a country, receive a background guide written by that committee's chairs outlining the topics to be debated, and spend the conference days in structured committee sessions culminating in a closing ceremony where awards are announced.
3.2 What the GIKI Moot Cup Is
A moot court competition is a simulated appellate court proceeding. Teams of two to four students are given a hypothetical legal problem (the “moot proposition”) months in advance, and must research the applicable law, write formal legal briefs (called memorials or memoranda) arguing both sides of the case, and then appear in person to deliver oral arguments before a panel of judges, who question them on the strength of their legal reasoning exactly as a real bench would. Unlike GIMUN's negotiation-based format, the Moot Cup is adversarial and rules-of-evidence-driven, typically organized into problem categories or tracks (for example, constitutional law, criminal law, or international law problems), preliminary rounds, quarter/semi-finals, and a final round judged by senior legal practitioners or academics. Awards typically include Best Team (Champions and Runners-up), Best Memorial, and Best Oralist.
3.3 Why One Website for Two Different Events
GIMUN and the GIKI Moot Cup are organized by the same committee, run on the same dates, at the same venue, and draw from overlapping applicant pools (many participating universities send both MUN delegations and moot teams). A shared website is the right call for three concrete reasons: first, it means one registration deadline reminder, one venue map, one accommodation and travel page, and one sponsor pitch serve both audiences instead of duplicating effort across two separate sites; second, it presents a single, more credible institutional brand to sponsors, faculty advisors, and press, rather than two smaller, less polished properties; and third, it matches how the Organizing Committee itself is structured — one team, one budget, one set of logistics, two competition formats layered on top.
The risk to manage is the opposite failure: making the site feel like it is only about one event with the other awkwardly squeezed in. Section 5 (Information Architecture) is built specifically to avoid that, using a shared shell with two co-equal, clearly branded event tracks rather than a primary event and an attachment.
3.4 The Core Problem This Website Solves
Today, a prospective delegate or moot participant trying to learn about the event has to piece information together from multiple, often inconsistent sources: a Facebook page for announcements, a Google Form for registration, a WhatsApp group for last-minute schedule changes, and PDF attachments emailed out for handbooks and background guides. This creates four recurring problems the Organizing Committee has to absorb: repeated inbox and DM questions asking things already “answered somewhere,” confusion about where the authoritative version of a schedule or handbook lives once it has been revised, a weaker first impression for sponsors and faculty advisors sizing up whether the event is professionally run, and lost participants who simply give up looking for the right form or document and don't register at all. The website specified in this PRD is built to directly close each of those four gaps.
4. Purpose and Objectives of This Document
This PRD exists to remove ambiguity before a single line of code is written. It defines what the website must do, for whom, with what content, built on what technical foundation, delivered on what timeline, and validated against what acceptance criteria — so that the person building it (the Organizing Committee lead) can work from a single reference instead of reconstructing decisions mid-build. Give every prospective and confirmed participant one authoritative place to find registration, committee/case assignments, background guides and handbooks, the live schedule, and venue logistics — eliminating the need to search Facebook, WhatsApp, and email threads.

6. Scope
6.1 In Scope
A single responsive marketing + content website covering both GIMUN and the GIKI Moot Cup, on one domain, one shared navigation shell.
Dedicated content sections for each event: committees & country matrix (GIMUN) and problem categories & rules (Moot Cup).
A form-only registration flow for both events (delegation and individual paths for GIMUN; team-based registration for the Moot Cup) — no payment collection anywhere on the site.
A resource hub / document library for handbooks, background guides, rules documents, and templates, organized and searchable/filterable by event and category.
A day-by-day schedule / agenda view covering both events, filterable by track.
A secretariat / organizing team page, sponsors & partners page, FAQ, venue & travel/accommodation page, and a gallery/press page for past-edition photos and media coverage.
Live-day features: an announcements/updates feed and a results & awards page populated after the event.
Lightweight, file-based content maintenance workflow suited to a single non-professional-developer maintainer.
Basic analytics, SEO fundamentals, and accessibility baseline (see Section 12).
6.2 Out of Scope (For This Version)
Any online payment collection or invoicing — explicitly excluded per the Organizing Committee's direction. Registration is form submission only; fee collection and confirmation happen manually, outside the website.
A logged-in delegate/participant portal with authentication (e.g., a MUNager-style system where each delegate logs in to see personalized assignments). This is listed as a strong Phase 2 candidate in Section 16, not Phase 1.
Real-time committee tools (live voting, resolution submission portals, crisis-simulation update feeds during committee sessions).
Multi-language support beyond English.
Native mobile apps — the responsive website serves mobile users directly.
Automated email/SMS notification systems beyond simple form-submission confirmation emails.
Livestreaming or video hosting infrastructure (embedding existing YouTube/social links is in scope; hosting original video is not).
Why payment is explicitly excluded
The Organizing Committee has directed that no payment flow appear anywhere on the site: registrants submit the form, and the Organizing Committee follows up manually (typically to arrange bank transfer or in-person payment and confirm the seat). This is a deliberate scope reduction that also removes an entire category of security and compliance work (no card data, no PCI concerns, no payment-gateway integration or fees) — worth calling out explicitly so it is never accidentally reintroduced mid-build.

10. Competitive and Inspirational Analysis
Before specifying features, existing MUN and moot court conference websites were reviewed to identify patterns that work well and pitfalls to avoid. This section summarizes the findings and states, explicitly, which patterns were adopted, adapted, or deliberately rejected for this project.
10.1 What the Strongest MUN Conference Sites Have in Common
Long-running, large-scale MUN conferences (Harvard WorldMUN, Harvard National Model United Nations (HNMUN), and Harvard Model United Nations (HMUN)) were reviewed as reference points, since they represent decades of iteration on exactly this kind of site. Across all three, the same structural pattern recurs: a public-facing marketing layer and a separate, more transactional registration/logistics layer.
A Secretariat/leadership welcome letter on the homepage or About page, personalizing the event and establishing credibility before any logistics are presented.
A dedicated, structured registration and delegate-management system (Harvard's conferences use a custom tool called “MUNager”) that is clearly separated from the marketing site — delegates create an account, submit delegation details, and later receive committee/country assignments and invoices through it.
A public FAQ page addressing eligibility, fees, what's included, and logistics — reducing inbound questions by answering them once, visibly, instead of repeatedly by email.
Clear, prominent statement of dates, venue, and the registration deadline near the top of the homepage — the single most time-sensitive piece of information is never buried.
A committees page listing every committee with its type (General Assembly, Specialized Agency, Crisis Committee, etc.) and a background-guide download per committee, rather than one bundled document for the whole conference.
Sponsor/partner acknowledgment and a media/gallery presence from past years, used as social proof for both prospective delegates and future sponsors.
Clear eligibility rules stated up front (age or enrollment requirements), preventing wasted registrations from ineligible applicants.
10.2 What the Research Confirmed Should Be Different Here
A full account-based delegate portal (like MUNager) is the right long-term pattern but is explicitly out of scope for this version, given the no-payment, form-only registration decision and the single-maintainer constraint — see Section 16 for this as a Phase 2 recommendation once the site and process have proven themselves for one cycle.
None of the reference sites had to represent two structurally different competition formats (negotiation-based MUN vs. adversarial moot court) under one roof — this is a genuinely different information-architecture problem, addressed in Section 11, not something to copy directly from a single-event reference site.
Reference sites are built for large, well-resourced, multi-person web teams. This PRD deliberately scales the ambition down to what one maintainer can sustain, favoring a simpler, file-based content approach over a heavier custom admin system (Section 13).
10.3 What Moot Court Competition Sites Emphasize Differently
Moot court competitions place much greater weight on precise, unambiguous rules content than MUN sites do, because memorials and procedural compliance are graded formally and a missed deadline or formatting rule can disqualify a team. Reviewed moot competitions (referenced generally, since individual sites vary widely) consistently foreground: the moot proposition or problem itself as a prominent, clearly dated download; a strict rules/rules-of-procedure document covering memorial format, word count, citation style, and submission deadlines; a clarifications/queries mechanism where teams can ask questions about the proposition and see all answers published centrally (so no team gets an information advantage); and a results/bracket structure once the competition begins. This directly informs the Moot Cup-specific requirements in Section 8.2.
10.4 Design and Brand Tone Reference
For the chosen “modern & vibrant, youth-oriented” direction, the goal is to feel closer to a well-produced student hackathon or youth summit brand than to the more institutional navy-and-serif look of the official United Nations or a law firm. That means: bold accent color used deliberately (not desaturated “safe” corporate blue), confident sans-serif typography, photography-forward sections showing real students in real committee/courtroom moments rather than stock diplomacy imagery, and motion/interaction touches (hover states, subtle transitions) that read as contemporary rather than dated. Section 6 translates this into a concrete design system.

12. Information Architecture
The site uses a shared shell (global navigation, footer, homepage, and truly shared content like venue/travel and sponsors) with two co-equal event tracks branching off it. A visitor should be able to tell within one click whether they are looking at GIMUN content or Moot Cup content, and switch between them without losing their place. This structure directly answers the risk raised in Section 10.2 — neither event should read as the “main” one with the other bolted on.

12.4 Why This Structure
Two decisions here are deliberate and worth explaining rather than leaving implicit. First, Resources and Schedule are top-level nav items rather than being nested under each event, because both are cross-cutting: a delegation head or faculty advisor bringing people to both events benefits from one combined schedule and one combined document library instead of checking two separate sub-sites. Second, Registration is a persistent button rather than a nav dropdown item, because it is the single highest-value action on the entire site (Section 5's #1 confirmed priority) and should never be more than one click away regardless of what page a visitor is currently reading.

“As a prospective delegate, team, or delegation head, I want to submit my details in one clear form suited to my situation (individual vs. group), and know for certain it was received — with a clear understanding that a person, not a payment page, will follow up with me next.”

Applicant type selector: Individual Delegate vs. Delegation (school/university group) — form fields adapt based on this choice, directly addressing Persona D's frustration in Section 9.4 (a delegation head needs to submit a group, not repeat the individual flow N times).

Client-side + server-side validation on required fields and email format before submission is accepted.

Confirmed registration deadline(s) per track

20. Content Maintenance / Admin Workflow
Because the site is fully custom-coded and maintained by one person (Section 2, Section 9.7's Persona G), this section specifies how that person actually updates content day-to-day — this is a feature in its own right, even though it has no public-facing UI.
Recommended Approach

The maintainer edits these files directly and redeploys, which for most modern hosting (Section 13.4) is a single command or an automatic action triggered by saving the change.

22. Key User Flows
22.1 Flow: Individual Delegate Registers for GIMUN
Arrives on Home, clicks 'Register' in the persistent nav button.
Track chooser presents GIMUN vs. Moot Cup; selects GIMUN.
Selects 'Individual Delegate' applicant type.
Fills personal details, selects committee preferences from the live Committee list, submits.
Sees on-page confirmation restating: 'no payment required now, our team will follow up.'
Receives confirmation email with the same restated details.
Organizing Committee receives a notification and later follows up manually with acceptance and payment instructions.
22.2 Flow: Delegation Head Registers a Group for GIMUN
Arrives via /gimun page's Register CTA (skips the track chooser since track is already known).
Selects 'Delegation' applicant type.
Enters delegation-level details, then adds each delegate via the repeatable member block (or uploads a spreadsheet for a large delegation).
Submits once for the whole group.
Receives one confirmation email listing every delegate submitted, for their own records.
Organizing Committee receives one notification containing the full delegation, rather than N separate individual notifications to piece together.
22.3 Flow: Moot Cup Team Checks for Proposition Clarifications
Arrives on /moot-cup/clarifications directly (bookmarked after registering) or via the Moot Cup nav dropdown.
Scans the numbered, dated list for any question relevant to their proposition concern.
If not already answered, uses the submission mechanism to ask a new question.
Organizing Committee answers via email/internally, then manually publishes the Q&A to this page, visible to all teams.
Team returns closer to the submission deadline to re-check for new entries.
22.4 Flow: Maintainer Posts an Urgent Schedule Change During the Event
A committee room change is decided on the morning of Day 2.
Maintainer edits the relevant Schedule Item's location field and sets its updated_flag to true in the schedule content file (or the connected Sheet, per Section 20's alternative).
Maintainer also adds a new Announcement entry describing the change.
Redeploys (or, if using the Sheet-based approach, the change reflects automatically without a redeploy).
The Schedule page shows the room change with an 'Updated' tag; the Homepage banner and Announcements feed both show the new announcement automatically, from the single entry.
22.5 Flow: Prospective Sponsor Evaluates the Event
Arrives at the Sponsors page, likely via a direct link sent in outreach rather than organic navigation.
Reads the 'why sponsor us' section with concrete reach numbers.
Downloads the sponsorship pitch deck.
Browses the Gallery for evidence of past-edition scale and quality (or, for a first edition, browses venue photography and organizing team credentials instead).
Uses the Contact page (Sponsorship query type) or the deck's stated contact to follow up.

24. Technical Architecture and Hosting Recommendation
24.1 Guiding Constraint
The build approach is confirmed as fully custom-coded, maintained by one person. The single most important technical decision this document can make, therefore, is not which framework is most powerful — it's which combination keeps ongoing maintenance realistic for that one person, especially under the time pressure of event week. Every recommendation below is filtered through that lens first.
24.2 Recommended Stack
Layer
Recommendation
Why
Frontend framework
A modern React-based framework such as Next.js (or a lighter static-site generator such as Astro if the maintainer prefers minimal JavaScript)
Both support the file-based content approach from Section 20 naturally, generate fast static pages by default (helping the performance NFR), and have large communities/documentation, which matters for a solo maintainer who will hit unfamiliar problems and need to self-serve answers
Styling
A utility-first CSS approach (e.g., Tailwind CSS) built against the design system in Section 14
Speeds up building the many similar Content Card / badge / button components consistently without hand-writing repetitive CSS
Content storage (frequent-update content)
Structured JSON/Markdown files in the repo, per Section 20's table, OR a connected Google Sheet for Schedule/Announcements specifically (Section 20's alternative)
Matches the single-maintainer constraint; avoids standing up and securing a full database + admin panel for content that doesn't need one
Registration & contact form handling
A managed form-backend service (form submissions routed to email + a spreadsheet/lightweight database) rather than a custom-built backend server
Removes the need to build, host, and secure a full backend just to receive form submissions — directly serves the no-payment, keep-it-simple registration flow in Section 17.2
Document/file storage
Static files served from the same hosting platform, or a simple cloud storage bucket for larger PDFs
Keeps background guides, propositions, and handbooks fast to load and simple to update by replacing a file
Hosting
A static/JAMstack-friendly host with a generous free tier (e.g., Vercel, Netlify, or Cloudflare Pages)
Matches the confirmed situation (domain owned, hosting not yet arranged): these platforms connect directly to a code repository, deploy automatically on every content update/redeploy (directly supporting the maintenance flows in Section 22.4), require no server administration, and their free tiers comfortably cover a medium-scale event site's traffic

24.3 Deployment Workflow
Code lives in a version-controlled repository (e.g., GitHub).
The chosen hosting platform is connected directly to that repository.
Every time the maintainer pushes a change (whether a new feature or a one-line content-file edit for an announcement), the hosting platform automatically rebuilds and republishes the live site within roughly one to two minutes.
The custom domain (already secured) is pointed at the hosting platform via a DNS change, which is a one-time setup step.
24.4 Why Not a Heavier Approach
A traditional server-rendered application with its own database and a custom-built admin dashboard was considered and rejected for Phase 1, specifically because it introduces ongoing server maintenance, security patching, and hosting cost that don't have a clear payoff given a single maintainer and a no-payment, content-hub-first feature set. That heavier approach becomes worth revisiting specifically if/when the Phase 2 delegate-portal idea in Section 26 is greenlit, since a login-based, personalized-assignment system genuinely needs a database and user accounts in a way the current scope does not.
24.5 Local Environment Notes
Standard modern web development requires Node.js and a package manager (npm or yarn) installed locally — mentioned here explicitly since the maintainer is working solo and may be setting this up for the first time.
Environment variables (e.g., the email address form submissions notify) should be stored in the hosting platform's environment-variable settings, not hard-coded into the repository, so the maintainer can change the notification address without a code change.

26. SEO and Marketing Considerations
Since most traffic will likely come from direct shares (WhatsApp, Instagram, email) rather than organic search, Open Graph/Twitter Card tags (title, description, preview image) per page are a higher-leverage investment than deep SEO keyword work — get link-preview rendering right first.
Still worth doing at low cost: descriptive, unique page titles (e.g., 'Committees — GIMUN 2027' not just 'Committees'), a sitemap.xml submitted to Google Search Console, and clean, readable URLs (already reflected in the sitemap in Section 12.2).
Consider a short, memorable, shareable URL structure for the registration link specifically (e.g., a redirect like /go or /apply pointing to /register) since this is the single link most likely to be shared directly in outreach messages and social bios.
27. Analytics and Success Metrics
Recommend a lightweight, privacy-respecting analytics tool (e.g., Plausible, Fathom, or Google Analytics 4 configured minimally) tracking, at minimum: page views per top-level page, registration-form start vs. completion rate (the single most important funnel on the site), resource-hub document download counts, and traffic source (direct/social/search) to understand which outreach channels are actually working. These map directly back to the success criteria table in Section 5.2, so the same numbers used to judge whether the site worked are the ones being tracked from day one.
28. Security and Data Privacy
28.1 What Data the Site Collects
Even with no payment collection, the site collects real personal data: names, emails, phone numbers, institution names, and dietary/accessibility notes through the Registration and Contact forms. This still requires basic, responsible handling.
28.2 Minimum Requirements
HTTPS enforced site-wide (standard on any modern hosting platform recommended in Section 24, and free).
A short, plain-language privacy note (not necessarily a formal legal privacy policy, but at minimum a sentence in the footer or on the Registration form) explaining what data is collected and that it's used only to process the application and contact the applicant.
Registration data should only be accessible to the Organizing Committee, not publicly exposed anywhere on the site (the public-facing Country Matrix on Committee pages, for example, should show which countries are taken, not the personal details of who took them).
Basic spam/bot protection (Section 23) on public forms, since an unprotected public form will eventually attract spam submissions that bury real registrations.
A sensible data-retention practice: since this is a recurring annual event, decide (even informally) whether past years' registration data is kept for continuity or cleared after each cycle, and treat dietary/accessibility notes as sensitive information not to be shared beyond what's operationally necessary.
Scope note
Because there is no payment processing, this project avoids PCI-DSS and payment-gateway compliance entirely (Section 6.2). The requirements above are the standard, lightweight baseline for any form-collecting site, not a heavier compliance regime — deliberately proportionate to the project's actual scope.

30. Testing, QA, and Launch
30.1 Testing and QA Plan
Cross-device check: real testing on at least one mid-range Android phone (the most common device profile for Persona A/B in this context), one iPhone, and desktop Chrome/Safari — not just browser dev-tools device emulation.
Form testing: submit real end-to-end test registrations for every applicant type (individual GIMUN, delegation GIMUN, Moot Cup team) and the Contact form, confirming both the applicant confirmation email and the Organizing Committee notification arrive correctly.
Link/document audit: every download link across Committees, Problem Categories, Rules, and the Resource Hub opens the correct, current file — this is the single highest-consequence content bug given how central these documents are (Section 16, 17.1).
Accessibility spot-check: keyboard-only navigation through the main nav and the Registration form; run an automated contrast checker against the palette in Section 14.1, particularly white text on the orange accent and teal accent, which are the combinations most likely to fail AA contrast at smaller text sizes.
Performance check: run the key pages (Home, Register, Resources, Schedule) through a page-speed tool on a throttled connection profile and confirm the sub-3-second target from Section 23.
Spam/bot check: confirm the honeypot/CAPTCHA protection on public forms actually blocks a basic automated test submission.

30.3 Event-Week Operating Mode
During the event itself, the site shifts from a mostly-static marketing asset to an active operational tool (Schedule and Announcements pages in particular). The maintainer should plan to check and be ready to update the site at least once each morning and once each evening of the event, with the ability to push an urgent update (per the flow in Section 22.4) within minutes if something changes on the ground — a room change, a delay, a safety notice. It is worth deciding, before the event starts, who else on the Organizing Committee can reach the maintainer quickly if an update is needed while they are not immediately available, since the maintainer is a single point of failure for this capability by design (Section 2).
